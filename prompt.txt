ROLE AND OBJECTIVE

Act as a multidisciplinary team consisting of a professional scientific 3D artist, entomologist, comparative anatomist, Three.js/WebGL engineer, scientific illustrator, and UI/UX designer.

Create a complete, publication-quality interactive 3D Butterfly Anatomy Atlas for advanced biology education and scientific communication. This must be a real, working web application, not a static mockup.

CORE TECHNOLOGY

Use:
- HTML5, CSS3, JavaScript ES modules.
- Three.js for actual interactive 3D rendering.
- OrbitControls for intuitive rotation, panning, and zooming.
- GLTF/GLB loading for high-quality anatomical models when available.
- A reliable skeletal or procedural animation system for wing movement.
- Raycasting for selecting individual 3D anatomical structures.
- SVG or dynamically rendered HTML overlays for precise leader lines and labels.
- Optional GSAP for polished transitions.
- Vite for development and production builds.

Choose a robust implementation that can run locally. Include all source files, package.json, asset instructions, and a README. Never pretend that unavailable external assets already exist.

SCIENTIFIC SPECIES AND ACCURACY

Choose a clearly identified, scientifically documented butterfly species as the primary specimen. Prefer a species with well-documented external and internal anatomy and suitable high-quality 3D references.

Display its scientific name, taxonomic classification, and the source of the anatomical references.

Prioritize anatomical accuracy over visual spectacle. Use realistic proportions, segmentation, wing venation, appendages, exoskeleton, scales, and material properties. Do not create a cartoon, toy, fantasy insect, generic mechanical insect, or low-poly approximation.

If a fully photorealistic rigged 3D asset is unavailable, construct a credible 3D specimen from modular geometry and properly sourced assets. Clearly distinguish scientifically accurate modeled structures from schematic or illustrative representations. Do not describe a schematic as a photorealistic dissection.

VISUAL ART DIRECTION

- Pure white background (#FFFFFF).
- Sophisticated scientific editorial design suitable for a high-end biology journal.
- Photorealistic specimen with rich, naturally occurring pigments and structural colors.
- Vivid but biologically plausible color accents for anatomical layers.
- Realistic PBR materials, subtle surface roughness, natural reflections, delicate shadows, and studio-quality lighting.
- Clean, minimal typography with excellent readability.
- Generous white space, restrained UI chrome, precise spacing, and elegant information hierarchy.
- Thin, refined leader lines with small anchor points.
- No childish illustrations, cartoon eyes, oversized anatomy, excessive gradients, cluttered dashboards, or unnecessary decorative elements.

The butterfly must remain the visual centerpiece.

LAYOUT

Build a polished responsive interface with:

1. A slim header with the atlas title, species name, search, and view settings.
2. A large central 3D viewport.
3. Small, unobtrusive floating controls for rotation, zoom, animation, reset, and explode/reassemble.
4. A collapsible anatomy navigation panel organized by anatomical system.
5. Minimal floating labels connected to the actual selected structures.
6. A compact scientific information panel with the structure name, function, anatomical location, and references.
7. A discreet legend indicating visible, transparent, isolated, and schematic anatomical structures.
8. An unobtrusive timeline or progress indicator for guided dissection.

Keep the specimen large and visually dominant. Avoid covering the anatomy with text.

REAL 3D ANATOMICAL SPECIMEN

Create a coherent, detailed, three-dimensional butterfly with anatomically credible geometry and independent selectable components.

The specimen must be viewed from multiple angles. Support smooth orbiting, panning, zooming, perspective changes, and predefined anatomical camera views.

The model must not be a flat photograph placed in a 3D scene. Use actual 3D geometry, appropriate depth, realistic lighting, and independent components.

If the best available model is external, provide clear asset attribution, license information, download instructions, and a fallback strategy.

ANATOMICAL COVERAGE

Build a detailed hierarchical anatomy browser. Include all applicable structures supported by the selected species and reliable references.

A. External morphology
- Head, thorax, abdomen.
- Dorsal, ventral, lateral, anterior, and posterior views.
- Exoskeleton and major body regions.
- Segmentation and external landmarks.
- Sexual dimorphism when applicable.

B. Head and sensory organs
- Compound eyes, ommatidial organization where appropriate.
- Ocelli, if present in the selected species.
- Antennal scape, pedicel, flagellum, and segments.
- Antennal clubs or species-specific terminal structures.
- Labrum, labial palps, proboscis, and relevant mouthparts.
- Proboscis coiling and extension, where supported by the model.

C. Thorax and locomotion
- Prothorax, mesothorax, metathorax.
- Prothoracic, mesothoracic, and metathoracic regions.
- Forewings and hindwings.
- Forelegs, midlegs, hindlegs.
- Coxa, trochanter, femur, tibia, tarsomeres, pretarsus, and claws.
- Wing articulation and attachment regions.

D. Wings
- Forewing and hindwing, each independently selectable.
- Dorsal and ventral wing surfaces.
- Major veins using an appropriate recognized venation nomenclature.
- Costal, radial, medial, cubital, and anal vein systems as applicable.
- Crossveins and cells where identifiable.
- Wing margins, apex, termen, and tornus.
- Color patterns, eyespots, and pattern elements where relevant.
- Scales and scale arrangement.
- Wing membrane and supporting structures.
- Wing-base structures and relevant articulations.

E. Internal anatomy
- Flight muscles and their attachment regions.
- Digestive tract: foregut, crop where applicable, midgut, hindgut, and associated structures.
- Hemolymphatic system and dorsal vessel.
- Nervous system: brain, subesophageal ganglion, ventral nerve cord, and segmental ganglia.
- Tracheal respiratory system, tracheae, and spiracles.
- Reproductive system with male and female anatomical variants where appropriate.
- Fat body and other major documented tissues.
- Excretory structures, including Malpighian tubules.
- Additional structures supported by species-specific anatomical evidence.

Do not imply that every structure can be observed directly in an intact adult. Internal organs must appear through a clearly labeled virtual dissection, cutaway, or transparent-body view. Never invent the exact internal positions of organs without evidence.

3D EXPLODED DISSECTION

This is a central feature, not a decorative afterthought.

Implement a smooth, controllable exploded view in which anatomical components move apart in a logical order while preserving their spatial orientation.

Requirements:
- Begin with an intact butterfly.
- Progress from the whole organism to the major body regions.
- Separate head, thorax, abdomen, wings, legs, and antennae.
- Allow deeper dissection into substructures and organ systems.
- Provide a continuous explosion slider and predefined dissection stages.
- Keep the movement vectors anatomically sensible and avoid arbitrary scattering.
- Preserve meaningful relative orientation and visual correspondence.
- Add smooth easing, staggered movement, and clear transitions.
- Allow individual parts to be isolated, hidden, faded, or restored.
- Include a one-click reassembly animation.
- Prevent components from disappearing off-screen.
- Update leader lines and labels throughout the animation.

Support both exploded external anatomy and a separate internal virtual dissection mode.

REALISTIC WING-FLAPPING ANIMATION

Provide a prominent button labeled "Flap Wings".

The wing motion must be believable for a living butterfly, not a robotic hinge or a flat image rotating back and forth.

Requirements:
- Animate the forewings and hindwings with coordinated, species-informed motion.
- Use plausible wing articulation around the wing bases.
- Model the movement as a time-dependent three-dimensional rotation with appropriate phase relationships, amplitude, and easing.
- Avoid perfectly uniform sinusoidal movement that appears mechanical.
- Allow starting, stopping, and changing playback speed.
- Provide a slow-motion mode for observing wing kinematics.
- Add subtle body stabilization and appropriate antenna or leg movement only when justified.
- Prevent the wings from intersecting the body or each other unnaturally.
- Ensure the animation remains smooth under different frame rates.
- Allow the user to inspect the specimen from different camera angles while the wings flap.
- Explain that the animation is illustrative if it is not calibrated against measured species-specific kinematic data.

HOVER AND SELECTION MICRO-INTERACTIONS

Every selectable anatomical component should support:
- A subtle hover highlight.
- A refined outline or restrained emissive accent.
- A small tooltip with the anatomical name.
- A smooth camera focus transition when selected.
- A clear selected state that differs from hover.
- An animated leader line that connects the structure to its label.
- A concise information panel with scientific terminology and function.

Clicking a structure must select the actual 3D object using raycasting. Do not simulate selection with unrelated static buttons.

Use restrained, elegant motion. Avoid flashing, excessive bouncing, or distracting effects.

SCIENTIFIC LABELS AND DESCRIPTIONS

Each anatomical entry should provide:
- Preferred scientific name.
- Common name where applicable.
- Anatomical hierarchy.
- Location and relationship to neighboring structures.
- Primary biological function.
- Relevant morphological or physiological details.
- Species-specific notes where supported.
- Source citations or references.
- Confidence or evidence classification when useful.

Use concise labels on the 3D model and more detailed descriptions in the information panel.

Keep labels readable at every zoom level. Avoid line crossings, overlapping labels, and ambiguous anchor points. Dynamically reposition or hide labels when necessary.

Provide a search field that can locate an anatomical structure, focus the camera, highlight the component, and display its description.

GUIDED EXPLORATION

Create a guided, step-by-step dissection experience:

1. Whole butterfly overview.
2. External morphology.
3. Head and sensory structures.
4. Thorax and flight apparatus.
5. Forewings and hindwings.
6. Legs and appendages.
7. Abdomen.
8. Internal organ systems.
9. Comparative anatomy and summary.

Each step should have a short explanation, a suggested camera view, highlighted structures, and a next/previous navigation system.

Allow users to exit guided mode at any time and freely explore the model.

CAMERA AND TRANSITIONS

Implement:
- Smooth zoom in and zoom out.
- Focus-on-selection.
- Orbit around the selected structure.
- Predefined dorsal, ventral, lateral, anterior, and posterior views.
- Fit-to-specimen and reset-camera controls.
- Smooth transitions between intact, exploded, isolated, and internal views.
- Appropriate near and far clipping planes.
- Responsive camera behavior and sensible zoom limits.

Use easing that feels precise and scientific, not like a game menu.

COLOR AND MATERIAL SYSTEM

Maintain the white background while using a rich, coherent palette for visual differentiation.

Use realistic biological colors on the intact butterfly. For virtual dissection, use consistent, restrained colors that distinguish organ systems without implying false natural pigmentation.

Include a small legend. Preserve the original specimen colors whenever the user returns to the intact view.

Avoid assigning arbitrary colors to anatomical structures without indicating that the colors are visualization aids.

PERFORMANCE AND ACCESSIBILITY

- Target smooth rendering on typical modern desktop computers.
- Use sensible polygon counts, texture resolutions, and lighting.
- Load heavy assets progressively.
- Show loading progress and meaningful error messages.
- Handle missing assets gracefully.
- Dispose of unused GPU resources where appropriate.
- Pause expensive animation when the tab is not visible.
- Support responsive layouts and keyboard navigation.
- Include accessible labels and visible focus states.
- Respect reduced-motion preferences where feasible.
- Provide a static fallback if WebGL is unavailable.
- Do not let a failed external asset silently produce an empty viewport.

SCIENTIFIC INTEGRITY AND REFERENCES

Use reliable entomology textbooks, peer-reviewed research, museum collections, and documented scientific 3D assets.

Never fabricate citations, DOIs, experimental observations, species-specific measurements, or anatomical claims.

Separate verified anatomical information from explanatory visualization. Include an in-app bibliography and an asset attribution section.

Make clear which structures are directly modeled, which are simplified, and which are schematic.

FINAL QUALITY BAR

The finished product should resemble a premium interactive scientific museum exhibit combined with a peer-reviewed anatomical atlas.

It must combine genuine 3D depth, scientifically defensible anatomy, elegant visual design, and meaningful interaction. Scientific correctness and working behavior take priority over visual claims that cannot be supported.
